Finding undiagnosed diabetes without a blood test

ES 335 Machine Learning, IIT Gandhinagar, 2026. Aditya Priyadarshi. Code and data pipeline on GitHub.

About 27 in 100 US adults never told they have diabetes had an HbA1c of 5.7% or more in 2017-2020. Sending 43 in 100 adults for a blood test, the ADA's seven-question risk test found 69% of those 27. A model trained on the 2007-2014 surveys sent 42 in 100 and found 71%, a gain too small to tell apart from chance. The USPSTF 2021 rule sent 45 in 100 and found 61%.

The question

Using only what an adult can answer and a nurse can measure with a scale and a tape, which way of choosing people for an HbA1c test finds the most undiagnosed dysglycaemia per test on a later survey: the ADA risk test, the USPSTF 2021 rule, the ADA 2026 rule, or a model trained on 2007-2014?

Prediabetes and early type 2 diabetes cause no symptoms, so they are found only when someone orders a blood test. Every screening rule is therefore a decision about who gets tested. A useful answer says how many more cases a method finds for the same number of tests, with an honest interval, on people it has never seen.

One row is one US adult aged 20 or over, examined in NHANES, who has never been told they have diabetes and takes no diabetes medicine: the people a screening rule is meant for. The outcome is that adult's laboratory HbA1c: 5.7% or more (prediabetes or diabetes range, the primary target) and 6.5% or more (diabetes range). The inputs are only things known before any blood test.

The project tests five hypotheses:

  • H1 (the only confirmatory one). At the ADA risk test's number of tests, the LightGBM classifier finds more adults with HbA1c 5.7% or more. Supported only if the 95% interval of the paired difference in sensitivity excludes zero.
  • H2. For HbA1c 6.5% or more, predicting the HbA1c value beats the yes/no classifier.
  • H3. Any gain can be split into new weights, more inputs, learned curves and interactions.
  • H4. Calibration drifts across survey years.
  • H5. For Asian adults, rules with a BMI cut-off of 25 miss more cases than BMI 23 variants and the models.

The plan, hypotheses and every analysis choice were fixed in a pre-registration before any model was trained, and the models were frozen in FROZEN.md before the test survey's HbA1c values were read.

Data

The National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES, CDC) interviews and examines a nationally representative sample of US residents every two years and measures HbA1c in a laboratory. Six cycles are used, from 2007-2008 to the 2017-March 2020 pre-pandemic file, downloaded straight from the CDC with a checksum for every file.

One row is one adult aged 20 or over who was examined, is not pregnant, has an HbA1c result, has never been told they have diabetes and takes no diabetes medicine. Of 66,148 participants, 29,394 adults meet these rules: 18,456 in 2007-2014 (training), 4,294 in 2015-2016 (calibration) and 6,644 in 2017-March 2020 (test).

Key variables: laboratory HbA1c (LBXGH, the outcome); measured height, weight, waist, arm circumference and upper-leg length; and questionnaire answers on blood pressure, family history, gestational diabetes, heart disease, smoking, activity, education and income. Notes on the data

  • The 2017-March 2020 file. NHANES stopped fieldwork in March 2020 because of COVID-19. CDC combined the partial 2019-2020 sample with 2017-2018 into one nationally representative "pre-pandemic" file (prefix P_), with its own exam weights (WTMECPRP) that are valid only for the combined file. It is used as one test survey, with those weights and its own 24 strata and 49 survey areas.
  • Top-coding. Age is top-coded at 80 (everyone 80 or older is recorded as 80) and the income-to-poverty ratio at 5. Both enter the models as recorded, so the models cannot tell 80 from 90, or a ratio of 5 from 10.
  • Asian adults. NHANES reports non-Hispanic Asian adults as a separate group only from 2011-2012 (RIDRETH3); earlier they are counted under "other race". The models use no race at all, the BMI 23 variants of the rules can only be applied from 2011 on, and the Asian-adult results (H5) come from the test survey.
  • Checks against published numbers. Before any model was trained, the baselines were checked against studies of the same surveys. The ADA score's AUC for HbA1c 5.7% or more is 0.73 in 2009-2010 and 0.72 in 2011-2012, against 0.72-0.74 in Poltavskiy et al. (2016) for 2009-2012. USPSTF 2021 eligibility in 2017-March 2020 is 44.7%, against 43.8% in O'Brien et al. (2023), who used fasting adults only.

Details in the repository: download script and file manifest, cohort rules, week-1 data checks.

Preprocessing

  • Twelve NHANES files per cycle are merged on the participant id. Answers coded refused or don't know become missing, never "no", because treating them as "no" would hide uncertainty in a risk factor.
  • Questions asked only of some people are filled from the skip pattern, because the answer is implied: men and women never pregnant have no gestational diabetes; people never told of high blood pressure take no blood-pressure medicine.
  • Adults taking insulin or diabetes pills are excluded even if never told they have diabetes, because they are already treated and no screening rule is meant for them. "Borderline" answers stay in, since those are people screening should find; a sensitivity analysis drops them.
  • Variable names that changed between cycles (for example the diabetes-pills question in 2007-2008) were checked against the codebooks.
  • Inputs: age, sex, BMI, waist, waist-to-height ratio, height, weight, arm circumference, upper-leg length, told high blood pressure, blood-pressure medicine, family history, heart disease or stroke, gestational diabetes, smoking, leisure-time activity, weekly activity minutes, sitting time, education and income-to-poverty ratio.
  • Never used as inputs, each for a reason: blood values (not known before the test being decided); measured blood pressure (the device changed in 2017); "told you have prediabetes" and "blood test in the past three years" (they reveal earlier testing, which the leakage ladder measures); race and ethnicity (HbA1c reads higher in Black adults at the same glucose, so race would partly predict the measurement, and the ADA test has none either); survey weights and the survey year (unknown for a new patient).
  • Missing values are rare (0-5% for body and history items, 8-14% for income). Trees use native missing-value handling; logistic models use median imputation with missing flags, fitted on the training cycles only.

HbA1c itself moves between survey cycles: the share at 5.7% or more ranges from 23.6% to 28.6% after age standardisation, without a clean step at the 2013 lab change. That is why every model is recalibrated on 2015-2016, which used the same laboratory and analyser as the test survey.

Details in the repository: recoding of every variable, model inputs, decisions log.

LightGBM and L1 settings were chosen by rolling-origin tuning; the EBM settings were fixed in advance. Every model is recalibrated on 2015-2016 with Platt scaling. Methods: L1 logistic regression (Tibshirani 1996), EBM (Lou et al. 2013; Nori et al. 2019), LightGBM (Ke et al. 2017), the value model's distributional approach (Peacock et al. 2012) and Platt scaling (Platt 1999); see References. Code: rules and ADA points, models, tuning and calibration, full tuning grid.

The chosen LightGBM settings sat on the edge of the pre-registered grid (15 leaves; minimum leaf 50 and L2 penalty 5 for 5.7%+, minimum leaf 200 for 6.5%+). A check added after review tried settings beyond those edges, tuned the same way: the best one for 5.7%+ (7 leaves, minimum leaf 25, L2 20) lowers mean validation log-loss by 0.00004 (0.01%); the best one for 6.5%+ (15 leaves, minimum leaf 400, L2 0) lowers mean validation log-loss by 0.00039 (0.32%). The frozen models were kept.

How it was evaluated

  • Split by survey cycle. A screening tool is built once and used on later people, so models train on 2007-2014 (18,456 adults), are recalibrated and thresholded on 2015-2016 (4,294), and meet 2017-March 2020 (6,644) once. Settings were tuned on three forward checks inside 2007-2016, shown above; the exact cycles are in splits.json.
  • Same number of tests. A programme can test only so many people. Each model's threshold sends the same share of 2015-2016 adults for a test as the ADA risk test, the USPSTF rule or the ADA 2026 rule, and is then applied unchanged to the test survey. The main measure is sensitivity: the share of people with HbA1c at or above the cut-off who are sent for a test.
  • Other metrics. PPV (the share of tested adults who are cases) and tests per case found (1/PPV); ROC-AUC and PR-AUC for ranking, PR-AUC being better suited to the rare 6.5% target; and Brier score, observed/expected cases and calibration slope for calibration.
  • Survey design. Estimates use the NHANES weights, so they describe US adults. Intervals come from 1,000 Rao-Wu bootstrap replicates that resample survey areas within strata, and both methods in a comparison share each replicate (code: src/evaluate.py).
  • Leakage ladder. Published models report AUCs of 0.82-0.89 (Sun et al. 2026; Heshmat Feizabadi et al. 2026). The ladder below shows what happens on 2007-2016 when the honest setup is loosened one step at a time. Each bar is the mean over 20 random seeds and the line spans the lowest and highest seed; boosting rounds are chosen on a fifth of the training rows, never on the rows being scored. This was revised after an external review: the frozen version in FROZEN.md used one seed, which put the random split at the bottom of its range, and let step 1 reuse rounds tuned on its own test rows.

A random split on its own changes AUC by −0.004 on average, well inside its seed-to-seed range (0.752 to 0.782), so it shows nothing either way. The inflation comes from counting already-diagnosed adults as cases (+0.039) and from two questions that reveal past testing (+0.036); one blood value adds +0.004.

Only giving a linear model all the inputs instead of the seven ADA items (+3.0 points, +0.7 to +5.3) changed sensitivity by more than the noise. The other steps moved it by between −0.9 and +0.2 points, with intervals that include zero.

Predicting the HbA1c value instead of a yes or no (H2)

For HbA1c of 6.5% or more, predicting the HbA1c value (model B) instead of the yes/no label (model A) changed PR-AUC by −0.006 (95% interval −0.019 to +0.007) and sensitivity at the ADA test's share tested by 0.0 points (−6.0 to +6.3). There is no detectable difference, and with 237 cases in the test survey the comparison is imprecise.

Survey years (H4)

Left: share of adults without a diagnosis whose HbA1c is 5.7% or more, by survey. Right: observed over expected cases for the chosen model, out of time and before recalibration, and after recalibration on 2015-2016.

Before recalibration, every model expected more cases than were observed, in every later survey: observed/expected 0.75 to 0.95 for HbA1c 5.7%+ and 0.57 to 0.79 for 6.5%+. The main reason is that the models were trained without survey weights: 32% of the 2007-2014 sample has HbA1c 5.7%+, but 26% once weighted to US adults. A check added after review confirms most of this: trained with survey weights, the same LightGBM settings give observed/expected 0.87 to 0.99 for 5.7%+ on the three forward checks, against 0.79 to 0.92 unweighted, and 0.81 to 1.05 for 6.5%+, against 0.64 to 0.77, without better ranking (AUC changed by −0.012 to +0.002). The shortfall that remains, largest in 2013-2014, is drift between surveys. Recalibrating on 2015-2016 brought 2017-2020 to 0.97 for LightGBM (95% interval 0.92 to 1.03). For 6.5%+ the share rose from 2.0% to 2.4% between those two surveys, and the recalibrated LightGBM expected fewer diabetes-range cases than were found (observed/expected 1.15, 0.96 to 1.36). Calibration slopes ranged from 0.81 to 1.27, where 1 is ideal.

Groups of adults (H5)

Asian adults are the clearest gap (308 cases in the sample). The ADA test found 46% of their cases with the BMI 25 cut-off and 51% with BMI 23; the LightGBM classifier found 49% and the EBM 48%, without any race input. Among non-Hispanic White adults the ADA test found 76%. The USPSTF rule found 44% with BMI 25 and 60% with BMI 23, but the BMI 23 version also tests 43% of Asian adults instead of 30%. H5 holds in direction for every comparison, but only the USPSTF difference is clearly larger than the noise, and it comes with more tests.

Where the models did worse

Not every comparison favours the models. For HbA1c of 6.5% or more at the ADA test's number of tests, the ADA test found 85.1% of cases and every model found fewer (LightGBM 83.0%, −2.0 points, −8.6 to +4.1). At equal testing the differences run from −6.4 to +0.9 points, all with intervals that include zero. The refitted ADA items did clearly worse than the ADA points at their frozen threshold (−8.2, −16.1 to −0.2) and still trailed at equal testing (−6.4, −12.9 to +1.7): new weights for the same seven answers did not carry over to diabetes-range HbA1c in a later survey. By group (HbA1c 5.7%+, frozen thresholds), LightGBM found fewer cases than the ADA test among Mexican American adults (−4.8 points, −11.6 to +1.9) and non-Hispanic Black adults (−3.4 points, −6.7 to 0.0), and more among other Hispanic adults (+8.0 points, −0.1 to +14.9). These group comparisons were not pre-registered as tests, and with six groups and two models some intervals will exclude zero by chance.

Limitations

  • One HbA1c measurement is not a diagnosis; a clinic would repeat the test. Fasting glucose was not used to define cases.
  • NHANES represents the United States. The cut-offs, body shapes and risk factors may not transfer to India without new data.
  • The test survey has 2,345 adults with HbA1c of 5.7% or more and 237 with 6.5% or more. With the frozen thresholds the LightGBM interval rules out a gain above about 4 points; at equal testing it reaches about 6. Smaller gains cannot be told from zero, and the 6.5% comparisons are imprecise.
  • Several analyses on this page were added after the test, following an external review, and are marked where they appear. They read the test labels again, so they are exploratory, not confirmatory.
  • The ADA test is designed to be filled in at home with self-reported weight; here it is scored from measured weight, which flatters it slightly. With self-reported height and weight it tested 41% of adults and found 66% of cases, against 43% and 69%.
  • "Non-Hispanic Asian" in NHANES groups many origins, and its sample is small, so the Asian-adult intervals are wide.
  • Models were trained without survey weights and recalibrated with them; a weighted-training check was run after the review (see H4). Other pre-registered secondary analyses were cut for time and are listed in FROZEN.md.
  • HbA1c levels shift between survey cycles for laboratory and population reasons that this project cannot separate.

Conclusion

On a survey it had never seen, a LightGBM model built from the same kind of information as the ADA risk test, plus body measurements, found 70.5% of adults with HbA1c of 5.7% or more, against 69.2% for the ADA test. The difference, +1.3 points (95% interval −1.4 to +3.8), is too small to tell apart from zero, so the pre-registered hypothesis H1 is not supported. That test kept the threshold set on 2015-2016, which on 2017-2020 tested 1.5 points fewer adults than the ADA test. At exactly the same number of tests the models find +1.5 to +3.3 points more cases (LightGBM +2.6, −0.3 to +5.9). That analysis was added after the test, so it suggests a modest gain of about 3 points rather than proving one.

The models' AUC was higher (0.765 against 0.741 for the ADA points). Their higher share of cases among the people they tested mostly came from testing fewer people: at equal testing LightGBM's PPV is +1.6 points higher (−0.2 to +3.7). An EBM, a plain L1 logistic regression and gradient boosting did equally well; the only step on the methods ladder that clearly helped was giving a linear model all the inputs instead of the seven ADA items. Learned curves and interactions added nothing measurable.

The clearer result concerns today's screening rules. At the USPSTF 2021 rule's number of tests, LightGBM found 75% of cases against the rule's 61% (+13.9 points, +10.0 to +17.9), and the ADA risk test alone found more cases than the USPSTF rule while testing no more adults. The ADA 2026 rule finds 98% of cases, but only by testing 86% of adults.

The models are not better everywhere. For diabetes-range HbA1c (6.5% or more) the ADA test found 85% of cases against 83% for LightGBM, and the models found fewer cases than the ADA test among Mexican American and non-Hispanic Black adults, with intervals that include zero or only just exclude it. Asian adults remain the weak spot for every method except ADA 2026: about half of their cases are missed.

Honest evaluation matters. On the same 2007-2016 data, counting already-diagnosed adults as cases and adding two questions that reveal past testing lifts AUC from 0.771 to 0.843, into the 0.82-0.89 range that published models report; a random split on its own makes no reliable difference.

For a clinic choosing whom to send for an HbA1c test, the seven-question ADA risk test comes within about 3 points of a machine-learning model trained on 18,456 survey participants. The rule that leaves cases behind is the USPSTF age-and-BMI rule.

Reproduce

The README has the setup, run steps, data access, the AI-use disclosure and the citations. NHANES is public domain and needs no account: the download script fetches the 72 files from the CDC website. Raw data are not stored in the repository; the committed manifest lists every file's SHA-256, so any copy can be checked. Every step is a script, run in this order (there are no notebooks):

git clone https://github.com/adityapriyadarshi2025/nhanes-diabetes-screening
cd nhanes-diabetes-screening
pip install -r requirements.txt
bash scripts/download_nhanes.sh
python -m src.harmonise && python -m src.cohort
python -m src.fit
python -m src.ladder --original && python -m src.ladder
python -m src.test_once --force
python -m src.review_extras && python -m src.dev_checks
python -m src.export_web && python scripts/prerender.py

The download script checks every downloaded file against the committed SHA-256 manifest (--verify checks an existing copy without downloading), and src/harmonise.py reproduces the sealed-label hash recorded in the pre-registration. results/test/UNSEALED.txt is committed as the record that the test was run, so src.test_once refuses to run on a fresh clone without --force. scripts/recheck_h1.py recomputes H1 without the project's evaluation code.

Records: pre-registration, freeze record, decisions log and all result tables.

Changes since the proposal

  • Tuning uses three forward checks (rolling origin) instead of 2015-2016 alone; 2015-2016 still sets calibration and thresholds.
  • The USPSTF 2021 and ADA 2026 rules and a refitted ADA score were added as baselines.
  • An explainable boosting machine was added, and it runs the calculator instead of SHAP values.
  • Race and ethnicity were taken out of the models, because HbA1c reads higher in Black adults at the same blood glucose.
  • People taking insulin or diabetes pills were excluded even if never told they have diabetes.
  • Platt scaling replaced isotonic regression as the main calibration.
  • A leakage ladder and survey-year and subgroup analyses were added.

After the one-time test

Changes made on 15 Nov 2026 after an external review; each is dated in the decisions log. No model, threshold or confirmatory result changed.

  • The comparison at exactly the same number of tests now has intervals, and the PPV result is described as mostly a volume effect.
  • The 6.5% and subgroup results where the models did worse are discussed.
  • The leakage ladder is averaged over 20 seeds, with boosting rounds chosen on training rows only.
  • Two checks on development data were added: survey-weighted training (H4) and settings beyond the edge of the tuning grid.
  • Notes on the data and the checks against published numbers moved onto this page, and the results text is part of the page, so it reads without JavaScript.
  • The download script now verifies the committed manifest, the README explains --force, and PREREG.md separates its writing date from its first commit.
